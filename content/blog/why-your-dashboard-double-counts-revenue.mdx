Connect AdMob and Firebase to the same app and you now have two ad revenue figures. Connect Google
Ads and Apple Search Ads and you have two spend figures.

These look like the same situation. They need opposite handling, and a system that applies one
rule to both will be wrong in one direction or the other.

## Same money, described twice

AdMob bills the ads. Firebase, through GA4, observes the ad revenue events the SDK reports. Both
figures are legitimate and they will not match exactly — different attribution, different
processing, a few percent apart.

But they describe **one pot of money**. Sum them and your ad revenue roughly doubles.

The fix is precedence: pick one source and drop the other. The order is "closest to the money
wins" — the system that processes the transaction beats the analytics SDK observing it. AdMob over
Firebase for ad revenue. The store console over Firebase for in-app purchases, because the store
takes the payment.

Two details matter in the implementation:

**Choose once across the whole window, not per day.** Per-day selection fills gaps nicely and then
switches sources mid-series. Because two providers measure subtly different things, that draws a
step change into your chart that never happened in reality.

**Apply it at query time, not at write time.** Keep every provider's raw rows. When someone asks
why the number changed, the per-source history is the only thing that can answer.

## Different money, same measure

Google Ads and Apple Search Ads both report spend. That spend is on different networks, billed
separately, for different clicks. It is not one pot described twice — it is two pots.

Apply precedence here and you drop one network entirely. Connecting a second ad network would make
your reported total spend go *down*, which is the exact mirror image of the double-count bug and
considerably harder to notice, because a number getting smaller after you add a data source does
not look like a data-source problem.

Spend across ad networks is additive. So are paid installs and paid clicks.

## The model that handles both

Rank the sources in **tiers**. The first tier with any data wins; the rest are discarded. Sources
*within* a winning tier are summed.

- Ad revenue: `[[AdMob], [Firebase]]` — one or the other.
- Spend: `[[Google Ads, Apple Search Ads]]` — both, added.

Impressions show why the tiers need to be ordered rather than flat. Campaign impressions from the
ad networks are additive with each other. Store-listing impressions from Play Console are a
different measurement that happens to share a name — summing them with campaign impressions is
meaningless. Different tiers: ad networks first, store console only when no ad network is
connected.

## The third question: how does it collapse?

Precedence decides *which rows survive*. It does not decide how those rows combine across days and
campaigns, and that is a separate decision that is easy to get wrong by inference.

Summing works for counts and money. It is nonsense for ratios. Thirty days of a 40% conversion
rate is not 1200%. CPI averaged across two networks is a cost per install; CPI summed across two
networks is not a cost per anything.

The tempting shortcut is to infer this from the unit — average the percents, sum the rest. It does
not hold. ROAS is a percent that averages. CPI is a currency that averages. Installs is a plain
number that sums. Declare it explicitly per metric, because with inference every newly added ratio
metric gets silently summed until somebody notices a suspiciously large number.

## The test that keeps it honest

Assert that every metric has a declared source rule. An undeclared metric with two sources will
silently resume double-counting, and it will look like growth.
