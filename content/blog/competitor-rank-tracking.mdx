Competitive rank tracking is usually implemented as two separate measurements: look up your
rank for a term, then look up theirs. The resulting gap gets reported as a competitive
position.

It is not one. **The two numbers came from different moments**, and a search result page is
not stable between them.

## Why separate lookups are not comparable

Between your measurement and theirs, any of the following can change the page:

- A competitor's metadata update re-indexes.
- Ratings shift enough to reorder mid-page results.
- A paid placement appears or disappears.
- The store re-runs personalisation or an experiment.
- Simple ranking churn on a contested term.

None of these are exotic. On a competitive term, mid-page positions move within hours.

So a "gap" of four positions may be four positions, or two, or seven. You cannot tell,
because you never observed both apps in the same state of the world.

## The fix: read every position from one page

Search the term **once**, and record the position of every app you care about from that
single result set — yours and every tracked competitor.

The gap is then a real measurement rather than a subtraction of two unsynchronised numbers.
It costs strictly fewer requests than measuring each app separately, which is a rare case of
the correct method also being the cheaper one.

Alongside each position, record what you would need to interpret it later:

- the **storefront** and language
- the **scan depth** — as covered in [your keyword rank did not disappear, your scan ran
  out](/blog/why-keyword-rank-disappeared), a competitor "not in the top 100" is not the
  same as "ranked 150th"
- the **timestamp**
- ideally, the **full ordered result set**, so the comparison can be re-derived later
  without re-measuring

That last one is worth the storage. A stored result page lets you answer questions you had
not thought to ask — like which app took a position you lost — without a time machine.

## What to actually watch

Once positions are comparable, three things are worth tracking and one is not.

### Worth watching: overlap

**Which terms do you and a competitor both rank for?** That set defines who you are actually
competing with, as opposed to who you think you are.

Teams routinely discover their real competition is an app they had never tracked, because
competitor lists get built from market perception rather than from search results.

### Worth watching: gaps

**Terms they rank for and you do not.** This is the most actionable output of competitive
tracking. It is a list of demand you are not serving, filtered to terms already proven
relevant to your category by the fact that a similar app ranks there.

Not all of them are worth chasing — check relevance and market size — but it is a better
candidate list than brainstorming.

### Worth watching: movement, not position

A competitor sitting at position 3 for a year is context. A competitor moving from 30 to 8
in a fortnight is an event, and worth understanding: they changed metadata, ran a campaign,
got featured, or shipped something.

**Rate of change carries more information than absolute position.**

### Not worth watching: the aggregate "visibility score"

Most tools offer a single composite number blending rank across a keyword set. It moves for
too many reasons — a term entering or leaving your tracked set, a weighting change, one
head term moving — and cannot be traced back to a decision.

If a number cannot tell you what to do differently, it is a dashboard ornament.

## Choosing which competitors to track

Not the apps your marketing deck names. The apps that appear on your result pages.

A workable method:

1. Take your top 20 tracked terms.
2. Record the top 10 results for each.
3. Count which apps appear most often across those pages.

That frequency ranking is your real competitive set. It usually contains one or two apps
nobody internally considers a competitor, and omits one everybody does.

Re-run it quarterly. Competitive sets drift, and a tracked list that never changes is
tracking last year's market.

## Reading a competitor's metadata changes

Positions tell you *that* something moved. Metadata tells you *what* they did.

Snapshot competitor titles, subtitles and descriptions periodically. When a competitor's
rank moves sharply, the diff between snapshots frequently explains it — a new head term in
the subtitle, a repositioned title, a changed short description.

This is also the cheapest source of keyword candidates you will find, with the same caveat
as always: **their metadata is a supply signal, not a demand signal.** It tells you what
they decided, not what users search. Validate anything you borrow.

## The short version

- Two separate lookups produce a gap that is partly real and partly drift.
- Read every tracked app's position from **one** result page — more accurate and fewer
  requests.
- Store depth, storefront, timestamp, and ideally the whole ordered page.
- Track overlap, gaps, and rate of change. Ignore composite visibility scores.
- Build your competitor list from result pages, not from opinion, and refresh it quarterly.
- Competitor metadata explains rank movements and generates candidates — but validate them.
