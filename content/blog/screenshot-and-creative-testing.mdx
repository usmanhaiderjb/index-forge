Store page conversion is decided in the visible area before anyone scrolls or swipes. On both
stores that means the app icon, the name, and the first one or two screenshots.

Everything below that — the description, the remaining eight screenshots, the reviews section — is
read by a minority of visitors, and mostly by ones already leaning toward installing.

## What is actually visible

On the App Store, portrait screenshots show roughly **one and a half** in the first viewport;
landscape shows **one**. On Google Play it is typically **two and a half** portrait. The precise
number moves with device size and store redesigns, but the shape of the conclusion does not:
screenshots 4 through 10 are not doing the work you think they are.

Which makes the common mistake expensive: teams put their strongest feature on screenshot 5,
because that is the order the features appear in the product.

## Order for the swipe you will not get

1. **The core value, stated in text.** Not a bare UI shot — a caption a stranger can read in under
   two seconds.
2. **The single most differentiating feature.** The reason to pick you over the app above you in
   the results.
3. Onward from there, in descending order of persuasion.

Caption text matters more than the screen behind it. A device frame containing an unreadable
settings page is a wasted slot regardless of how good the app is.

## Test one thing

Google Play has native store listing experiments. iOS has Product Page Optimization. Both give you
a real split test against real traffic, and both are wasted if you change the icon, the first
screenshot and the subtitle in the same variant — you will get a result and no idea what caused it.

Change one element. Run it long enough to clear weekday and weekend effects, which in practice
means at least a full week and usually two.

## Read conversion rate, not installs

An experiment that runs during a campaign will show install lift that has nothing to do with the
creative. Store page conversion rate — installs divided by store page views — is the metric that
isolates the listing.

This is the same trap as judging ASO on total installs, at a smaller scale. Any figure that moves
with your ad budget cannot tell you whether a creative change worked.

## Localize the creatives, not just the text

Translating your description and leaving English screenshots is one of the most common half-done
localizations. The screenshots are what a visitor in that storefront actually looks at, and they
are still in a language they may not read.

If you are auditing localization coverage, note that measuring whether *fields are filled* will
score an untranslated storefront the same as a translated one. Both have a subtitle. Only one is
in the right language. The useful first question is not "how complete is this locale" but "is this
locale localized at all".
