Every app wants to rank for the two-word head term in its category. Almost none of them
should be trying yet.

"Habit tracker" is defended by apps with millions of ratings, years of engagement data, and
a conversion rate the store has been measuring since before you launched. Ranking there is
downstream of everything else going right, not a way to make it go right.

"Habit tracker for adhd" is winnable this quarter, converts better, and is one of the
inputs that eventually makes the head term reachable.

## Why the long tail converts better

A long-tail search is a **more specific statement of intent**, and specificity is what
converts.

Someone searching "habit tracker" might want streaks, might want reminders, might want
something for their kid, might be comparison shopping with no intention of installing
anything today. Your listing has to appeal to all of them and therefore appeals sharply to
none.

Someone searching "habit tracker with widget" has told you exactly what they want. If you
have it, your screenshots can say so, and the install follows.

This matters beyond the immediate conversion, because **conversion rate per term feeds
ranking**. As covered in [how app store rankings actually
work](/blog/how-app-store-rankings-work), the stores watch whether people who search a term
tap your result and keep the app. Ranking well on terms you genuinely serve compounds.
Ranking badly on terms you half-serve does the reverse.

## Why they are winnable

Three reasons, all structural:

1. **Fewer apps target them.** Most competitors spent their metadata on head terms, leaving
   specific phrases uncontested.
2. **Apple builds combinations for you.** With `habit`, `tracker` and `widget` distributed
   across your indexed fields, you are eligible for "habit tracker widget" without spending
   characters on the phrase. This is the single most useful mechanical fact in iOS keyword
   strategy.
3. **The bar is lower.** Ranking third for a term with modest volume needs far less
   authority than ranking twentieth for a head term — and third place on a small term
   produces more installs than twentieth on a large one.

## How to find them

### Alphabet-soup your seeds

Take a seed and walk the alphabet *after a space*:

```
habit a    habit b    habit c    ...
habit ta   habit tb   habit tc   ...
```

Typing "habit" returns terms starting with "habit". Typing **"habit t"** returns "habit
tracker", "habit tracker widget", "habit tracker for kids" — the multi-word searches you
would otherwise never see. This is the difference between a keyword list of forty terms and
one of four hundred.

### Mine your reviews

Your users describe your app in their own words, and their words are what they type into
search. Review text is a keyword source your competitors are almost certainly not reading.

Look for the phrases people use to describe **why** they installed. "I needed something
simple to track water" is a long-tail keyword wearing a sentence.

### Mine your support inbox

Same principle, higher signal. The questions people ask before installing are the searches
they ran.

### Look at what ASA surfaces

If you run Search Ads with broad match, the search-term report shows the exact long-tail
phrases users typed to reach you — with real impression and conversion numbers attached. See
[Apple Search Ads is the only real keyword volume data you can
get](/blog/apple-search-ads-keyword-data).

## How to judge which ones are worth it

Long tail is not automatically good. A term nobody searches is not a hidden opportunity, it
is a dead term.

Check three things:

**Does a market exist?** Search the term. If it returns almost no apps, that is usually
because nobody searches it — not because you found a gap. A result set in the single digits
is a warning, not an invitation.

**Is it evidenced?** A term you saw once in one autocomplete query is a guess. A term that
appears across several independent queries, or on both stores, is a finding. Weight
accordingly.

**Do you actually serve it?** "Habit tracker for adhd" is a promise. If your app has nothing
specific for that use case, you will get the install and lose the user — and post-install
churn feeds back into ranking.

## Turning a long-tail list into metadata

You cannot target four hundred terms. You do not need to.

**On iOS**, choose your vocabulary rather than your phrases. Because Apple combines across
fields, a well-chosen set of thirty single words in your keyword field covers hundreds of
combinations. Prioritise words that appear in many of your long-tail candidates —
`widget`, `reminder`, `streak`, `offline` — over words that appear in one.

**On Play**, the full description is indexed, so long-tail phrases can appear naturally in
prose. Two or three uses each, written for a human. A description that reads as a keyword
list is penalised and converts badly.

**In screenshots**, caption to the long tail. If "for adhd" is a term you serve and target,
a screenshot caption saying so converts that traffic far better than a generic feature list.

## The progression

The realistic sequence for a new app:

1. **Rank for your own brand.** Non-negotiable, and easy to lose if a competitor bids on it.
2. **Rank for three-word specific phrases.** Winnable in weeks.
3. **Accumulate conversion and retention data** on those terms.
4. **Move to two-word terms** as your authority grows.
5. **Contest the head term** — much later, and only if it is genuinely relevant.

Skipping to step five is the commonest ASO mistake, and it looks like doing nothing for a
year.

## The short version

- Head terms are defended by incumbents with data you do not have yet.
- Long-tail searches state intent, so they convert better — and conversion feeds ranking.
- Apple builds combinations across your fields, so target *vocabulary* on iOS, not phrases.
- Alphabet-soup with a space is how you find multi-word terms.
- A term with almost no competing apps is usually dead, not undiscovered.
- Only target what you actually serve; churn costs ranking.
