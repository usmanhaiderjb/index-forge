The native rating prompt is the highest-converting way to get reviews, and you get very few
of them. Both stores enforce quotas, neither tells you when a prompt was suppressed, and the
most intuitive way to raise your average is against the rules.

## The constraint nobody designs around

**iOS** allows roughly three prompts per user per year via `SKStoreReviewController`. Call it
more often and the extra calls do nothing — silently.

**Google Play's** In-App Review API has an undocumented quota, also enforced silently.

Two consequences follow, and the second is the one that breaks analytics:

**You get very few asks.** Three a year means the timing decision matters more than the copy,
the design, or anything else about the prompt.

**You cannot tell whether the prompt appeared.** Neither API reports it. Both complete
successfully whether the sheet was shown or suppressed.

That second point is worth being explicit about, because teams instrument around it and get
a number that means something other than what they think:

> An analytics event fired next to the prompt call records that **you called the API**. It
> does not record that a user saw anything.

Any funnel built on that event — prompts shown, prompt-to-review rate — is measuring your
own code path, not user behaviour. If you need a real denominator, you do not have one.

## When to ask

The principle: **ask after a moment of demonstrated success**, when the user has just
experienced the app working.

Good moments:

- A task completed that took effort — a workout finished, a budget balanced, a document
  exported.
- A streak or milestone reached.
- A returning session, several days in — someone who came back is someone who likes it.
- Immediately after a support interaction that went well.

Bad moments:

- **On launch.** The user has not experienced anything yet.
- **Mid-task.** You are interrupting the thing they opened the app to do.
- **After an error**, obviously, but also after any friction — a failed sync, a slow load, a
  paywall.
- **Immediately after a purchase.** They just gave you money; asking for a favour in the same
  breath reads badly.
- **On a schedule.** "Day 7" ignores whether day 7 went well.

The general test: *would this user, right now, describe the app positively to a friend?* If
you cannot tell, do not spend an ask.

## The rule people break

**Do not gate the prompt on sentiment.**

The pattern is familiar: ask "Enjoying the app?" first, show the store prompt to people who
say yes, and route the others to a support form.

This violates both stores' guidelines. It is review manipulation — you are filtering who is
allowed to leave public feedback, which is precisely what the rating is supposed to measure.
Enforcement is inconsistent, which is why the pattern persists, but the exposure is real and
it escalates to app removal.

Ask everyone, or ask nobody.

There is a legitimate adjacent pattern: make it easy to reach support **everywhere else in
the app**, so a frustrated user has an obvious non-store route. That is good product design
and it is not gating, because it does not condition the prompt on the answer.

## Raising the average without gating

If your rating is lower than the product deserves, the honest levers are:

**Fix the thing people are complaining about.** A rating is usually a product signal. Read
the one-star reviews and count the causes; the top one or two typically explain most of the
distribution.

**Time the ask better.** Asking at genuinely good moments shifts who responds without
filtering who is allowed to.

**Reply to reviews.** On Play, replying notifies the reviewer, and updated reviews after a
fix are common. See [responding to app reviews](/blog/responding-to-app-reviews).

**Accumulate volume.** A 4.2 from 40 ratings is fragile; a 4.2 from 40,000 is stable and
converts better than a 4.6 from 40. Volume is its own form of social proof.

**On Play, wait.** Play weights recent ratings more heavily, so a bad period decays out on
its own once the cause is fixed. This is the store working in your favour and it requires no
action beyond fixing the product.

**On iOS, consider a reset** — carefully. Apple lets you reset your rating on a new version.
It clears a damaged score, and it discards your review count with it. Worth it after a
genuinely bad release that has been fixed; not worth it for a mediocre average.

## Measuring what you can

Given you cannot measure prompts shown, measure the outcomes that are real:

- **Ratings per thousand active users**, over time. Blunt, but it responds to timing changes.
- **Rating distribution**, not just the average. A 4.3 made of fives and ones is a different
  product problem from a 4.3 made of fours.
- **Rating by app version.** This is what tells you a release caused a drop.
- **Rating by storefront.** Averages across markets hide a single-market collapse.

And be careful comparing periods across a version boundary on iOS if you have ever reset —
the series is not continuous, and nothing in the data marks the discontinuity.

## The short version

- iOS allows roughly three prompts per user per year; Play's quota is undocumented. Both
  suppress silently.
- Neither API tells you whether the prompt appeared — any "prompts shown" metric is fiction.
- Ask after demonstrated success, never on launch, mid-task, after friction, or on a
  schedule.
- Never gate the prompt on sentiment. It breaches both stores' rules.
- Volume stabilises a rating; Play heals on its own; an iOS reset costs your review count.
- Track distribution, version and storefront — not just the average.
