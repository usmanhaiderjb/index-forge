If you are measuring App Store Optimization by total installs, you are measuring your ad budget.

A campaign launch and a listing improvement look identical in that number. So does a campaign
pause and a ranking drop. The one figure everyone reports is the one figure that cannot tell you
whether the work you did had any effect.

## The metric you actually want

Organic installs — the ones you did not pay for. No store reports it. The console counts every
install; the ad network counts the ones it claims. Neither publishes the difference.

So you derive it:

```
organic = total installs − paid installs
```

That subtraction has three ways to go wrong, and all three produce a plausible-looking number.

## Trap 1: your total is already double-counted

If both Play Console and Firebase are connected, you have two install figures for the same app.
They are not duplicates in the sense of being identical — Firebase counts first opens, the console
counts installs, and they will differ by a few percent — but they describe the same event.

Sum them and your total is roughly doubled. Subtract paid installs from *that*, and organic comes
out wildly high. In our own test fixture, a de-duplicated total of 1000 with 400 paid installs
gives 600 organic. Summing two sources first gives 1540. Same inputs, two and a half times the
answer.

Pick one authoritative source for installs *before* subtracting. The store console beats the
analytics SDK, because the system that processes the install is closer to the truth than the one
observing it.

## Trap 2: no paid data does not mean no paid installs

The tempting shortcut when an ad account is not connected is to report organic as equal to total.
It is not. It is unknown.

An app with no connected ad account might be running no campaigns — or might be running three that
you cannot see. Declaring every install organic in that state is not a conservative default; it is
a confident wrong answer, and it will make a paid-driven month look like an ASO win.

There is a subtler version of the same mistake. An ad account connected six months ago, dark ever
since, still has paid install rows in the database. Check for paid data *anywhere* and you will
happily derive organic for last week, subtracting zero, and report the total as fully earned.

The check has to be scoped to the window you are deriving. Better still, to the span the ad
platform actually reported in: ad platforms omit zero-spend days rather than sending a zero, so a
gap *inside* the reported span genuinely means no paid installs that day, while a gap outside it
means you know nothing.

## Trap 3: attribution windows produce negative organic

Ad networks attribute on their own windows — a click seven days ago and an install today gets
claimed today. The console counts the install on the day it happened. On any given day, the
network can claim more installs than the console counted.

Subtract and you get a negative number. There is no such thing as −40 organic installs.

Floor it at zero, but record that you did. The discrepancy is real information: a day that floors
is a day where attribution and the console disagree materially, and a run of them means the
comparison is not trustworthy. Hiding that behind a clean zero throws away the signal.

## What to do with the split

Watch the *share*, not the count. Organic share tells you whether the listing is carrying more of
the load this month than last, independent of what the budget did. A rising total with a falling
organic share is a campaign working and a listing stagnating — which is a completely different
decision from the one the total install number would have suggested.

And when paid data is missing, show that it is missing. A blank is honest. A number that assumes
away the missing input is not.
