Keyword research for app stores is not keyword research for the web wearing different
clothes. The stores publish no search volume, index different fields, and give you a
different number of characters to work with. Most of what is written about ASO keywords
skips straight to tactics without saying where any of the numbers came from — which is
the part that decides whether the tactics are worth following.

This guide covers the whole process: where keyword data comes from, what "search volume"
means when nobody publishes it, how difficulty is estimated, and how to turn a list of
candidates into a metadata decision you can defend.

## The uncomfortable starting point: nobody publishes search volume

**Neither Apple nor Google publishes app store search volume.** Not through an API, not
in App Store Connect, not in Play Console. Every search volume figure you have ever seen
in an ASO tool is an estimate derived from indirect signals, however confidently it is
presented.

That is not a reason to give up on the number. It is a reason to know which signals
produced it, because the signals differ enormously in quality.

### What can actually be observed

| Signal | What it tells you | How good it is |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Store autocomplete | which terms the store suggests, **in popularity order** | strong, free, directly observable |
| Autocomplete position | that "habit tracker" outranks "habit journal" | strong for *relative* demand |
| Search result count | how many apps compete for a term | competitive density, not demand |
| Top charts | which apps rank in a category | context, not keyword demand |
| Apple Search Ads | **measured impressions per term** | ground truth, but only for your own account |

The single most useful free signal is **autocomplete ordering**. When you type "hab" into
the App Store, the suggestions come back ranked by how often people search them. That
ordering is a real popularity signal that Apple and Google hand you for nothing.

What it supports is a *relative index*: this term is searched more than that one. What it
does not support is "18,400 searches per month". You cannot derive an absolute figure from
an ordering, and any tool that shows you one has either calibrated against real
impression data or invented it.

### The one source of ground truth

**Apple Search Ads search-term reports contain measured impressions per term per
storefront.** Not an estimate — actual counts, from Apple.

If you run even a small ASA campaign, you are holding real volume data for the terms you
bid on. It is the only true volume number available to anyone outside Apple, and it is
worth mining before you trust any third-party estimate. Export your search-term report and
compare it against whatever your ASO tool claims. The gaps are instructive.

## How to build a keyword list

### Start from seeds, not from a blank page

Take ten to twenty words that describe what your app does in the language a *user* would
use, not the language your team uses internally. "Habit tracker", not "behavioural
adherence engine".

Then expand each seed through store autocomplete. Type the seed, note what comes back, and
type each of those. This is the same mechanism a crawler uses at scale, and doing it by
hand for twenty seeds takes an afternoon and teaches you more about your category than any
report will.

### Use the space trick for multi-word terms

Typing "habit" returns terms starting with "habit". Typing **"habit t"** returns "habit
tracker", "habit tracker widget", "habit tracker for kids" — the multi-word searches you
would otherwise never surface. Walking `seed + space + letter` through the alphabet is
where the long tail lives.

### Mine your competitors' metadata

Apps ranking in your category have made keyword decisions, and those decisions are
visible. Their titles and subtitles are the terms they chose to spend their most valuable
characters on.

A word of caution: **frequency across competitors is a supply signal, not a demand
signal.** It tells you what other developers believe, which is not the same as what users
search. Treat it as a candidate generator, then validate each candidate against
autocomplete.

### Watch for the boilerplate trap

Tokenise enough descriptions and you will find "contact us", "get started" and "free
download" appearing across half the category. They are shared because they are boilerplate,
not because they are keywords. A term is only interesting if a developer put it somewhere
the store actually indexes — which brings us to the biggest structural difference between
the two stores.

## iOS and Android index different things

This is the difference that invalidates most generic ASO advice.

**The App Store indexes:**

- App name (30 characters)
- Subtitle (30 characters)
- Keyword field (100 characters, invisible to users)
- In-app purchase names
- Developer name

**The App Store does not index your description.** Write it for conversion, because it
does nothing for discovery.

**Google Play indexes:**

- App title (30 characters)
- Short description (80 characters)
- **Full description (4,000 characters)**
- Developer name

Play has no separate keyword field. The description carries that load instead, which is
why Play listings read the way they do.

The practical consequence: **a term that appears once anywhere in your iOS metadata is
indexed.** Repetition does nothing. Apple even combines your title, subtitle and keyword
field into a single index and generates combinations — so "habit" in your title and
"tracker" in your keywords means you can rank for "habit tracker" without spending
characters on the phrase.

On Play, repetition has a mild effect and over-repetition is penalised. Aim for natural
usage two or three times across the description, not keyword stuffing.

## Judging difficulty

No store publishes keyword difficulty either. It is always a model, and a reasonable one
looks at the apps that currently rank:

- **How strong are the incumbents?** An app with two million ratings at 4.8 stars is not
  losing its position to a new entrant.
- **How deep is the result set?** A term returning eight apps is a different proposition
  from one returning two hundred.

The useful output is not a precise number, it is a stable ordering. If difficulty says
term A is harder than term B, and that holds consistently, you can prioritise. That is all
prioritisation needs.

### The trap: a thin result set is not an easy win

A term returning zero or two apps looks maximally easy by any difficulty model — nothing
is defending it. But **nothing ranks for it because nobody searches it.** An empty result
set usually means the term is a typo, a brand string, or a phrase no human types.

Before you celebrate an "easy" keyword, check that a market exists at all. Ten or more
competing apps is a reasonable floor for a term to be worth targeting.

## Turning a list into a decision

You now have candidates with a relative demand index and a difficulty estimate. The
decision rule is:

> **Chase terms with the highest demand you can realistically rank for**, given how
> established your app is.

In practice that means an opportunity score — demand discounted by difficulty. But three
adjustments matter more than the formula:

1. **Discount by evidence.** A term you observed once is a guess. A term confirmed across
   several independent queries, on both stores, is a finding. Do not let a single
   observation outrank a well-evidenced one just because its raw score is higher.
2. **Discount by market size.** As above — no competitors means no searchers.
3. **Weight by relevance, ruthlessly.** Ranking for a term your app does not serve produces
   installs that churn immediately, and both stores factor post-install engagement into
   ranking. An irrelevant keyword win is a ranking loss on a delay.

### Where to spend your characters

Given limited space, spend it in this order:

1. **Title** — the highest-weighted field on both stores. One or two head terms.
2. **Subtitle (iOS) / short description (Android)** — your second-strongest terms.
3. **Keyword field (iOS)** — everything else, comma-separated, no spaces, no repeats of
   words already in your title or subtitle, no plurals of words you already have.
4. **Description (Android only)** — natural usage of your secondary terms.

Never repeat a word across iOS fields. Apple indexes the union, so "fitness" in your title
and again in your keyword field wastes characters you could have spent on another term.

## Measuring whether it worked

Change your metadata, then wait. Both stores take days to re-index and re-rank, and
ranking is noisy at the best of times.

Two rules keep the measurement honest:

- **Change one thing at a time**, or you will not know which change moved the number.
- **Separate organic from paid.** A campaign launched in the same week will lift your
  installs whether or not your keywords improved.

Track rank per keyword daily, and record how deep the scan went. A keyword that "fell off
the chart" and one that fell out of a top-100 scan look identical in a report that does not
record its own depth.

## The short version

- Nobody publishes search volume; every figure is an estimate, so know its source.
- Autocomplete ordering is the best free demand signal, and it supports a *relative* index.
- Apple Search Ads impressions are the only ground truth available to you.
- iOS indexes title, subtitle and keyword field — **not** the description. Play indexes the
  description heavily.
- Difficulty is a model. Use it for ordering, not as a measurement.
- An empty result set is a dead term, not an easy one.
- Relevance beats volume, because irrelevant installs churn and churn costs ranking.
