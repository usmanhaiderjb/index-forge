Most app dashboards are wrong in small ways that compound. Not because the data is bad, but
because two stores, an ads platform and an analytics SDK all report overlapping numbers
using the same words for different things — and something has to decide which to believe.

This guide covers where the discrepancies come from, which source to trust for what, and
how to build a picture that survives someone checking it.

## The same word, four different meanings

"Installs" is the worst offender.

| Source | What it counts |
| --- | --- |
| App Store Connect | **Units** — first-time downloads, excluding redownloads |
| Play Console | **Installs** by user, or by device, or acquisitions — depending on the report |
| Firebase / GA4 | `first_open` events, which fire when the app is *launched* |
| Ad platform | attributed installs within a click window |

These will never match, and it is not a bug in any of them:

- A user who downloads and never opens the app is a store install and **not** a
  `first_open`.
- A user who reinstalls is a redownload in App Store Connect, possibly a new install in
  Play, and not a new `first_open` if analytics keeps their identity.
- An ad platform claims an install if it saw a click in the attribution window — including
  clicks from users who would have installed anyway.

**Pick one source per metric and stay with it.** A dashboard that sums installs across
sources is not showing you more installs; it is showing you the same installs more than
once.

## The revenue double-count

The commonest and most expensive version of this problem.

If your app has in-app purchases *and* ads, and you pull revenue from the store *and* from
your ad network, you may be adding two numbers that already overlap — or adding two that
should never have been added at all.

Store revenue is **proceeds**: after the platform commission, in the currency the store
paid you, on the store's fiscal calendar. Ad revenue is usually **gross estimated
earnings**, in your account currency, on a calendar day, and revised later.

Adding proceeds to estimated earnings produces a total that means nothing. Worse, it looks
plausible, so nobody checks it.

The fix is to keep them separate, label them, and only combine them at the point of
display, with the definition attached. See [why your dashboard double-counts
revenue](/blog/why-your-dashboard-double-counts-revenue).

## Store data is not final

Both stores restate figures after publishing them.

- Apple's data settles over roughly one to three days; early figures for the last day are
  routinely incomplete.
- Google's reports revise, particularly around refunds and cancellations.
- Ad networks revise estimated earnings into finalised earnings, and the two differ.

The practical consequences:

1. **Never compare a partial day to a complete one.** Yesterday will always look worse than
   the day before if yesterday is still filling in.
2. **Re-fetch a window, do not append a day.** A pipeline that writes each day once and
   never revisits it permanently records the first, incomplete answer.
3. **Record when a figure was fetched.** Without it, you cannot tell a genuine change from a
   restatement.

More on this in [store data is not final](/blog/store-data-is-not-final).

## Organic versus paid

The single most important split in app analytics, and the one most often skipped.

Total installs is the wrong number to judge ASO on, because paid spend lifts it regardless
of whether your listing improved. If you cannot separate the two, you cannot tell whether
your metadata work did anything.

Neither store hands you a clean organic number, and the derivation is not simple
subtraction — attribution windows overlap, some paid users would have found you anyway, and
browse traffic sits awkwardly between the two.

The workable approach is to derive organic as *total minus attributed paid*, keep the
derivation visible, and label the result as derived rather than measured. See [total
installs is the wrong number to judge ASO on](/blog/organic-vs-paid-installs).

## Traffic sources

Both stores report where an impression came from, and the categories are worth
understanding because they respond to entirely different work.

- **Search** — someone typed a term. This is what keyword optimisation moves.
- **Browse** — someone found you through charts, category pages, or editorial. This
  responds to velocity and featuring, not keywords.
- **Referral** — a link from outside the store. This is your marketing, not your ASO.

An app whose installs are 80% browse and one whose installs are 80% search need completely
different work, and a single install number hides that entirely.

The stores name these categories differently — Apple's Analytics Reports and Play Console
use different labels for roughly the same buckets — so any cross-store view has to
normalise them, and should say so.

## Conversion rate, carefully

Conversion rate is impressions to installs, and it is the cleanest measure of whether a
listing works. Two cautions:

**Never average an average.** Conversion rate for a period is total installs divided by
total impressions for that period — not the mean of the daily rates. Averaging daily rates
weights a quiet Sunday the same as a busy Monday.

**Segment by traffic source.** Search converts far better than browse, because search users
already told you what they wanted. A blended rate moves whenever your traffic mix moves,
even if neither segment changed.

## Building a dashboard that holds up

What separates a dashboard people trust from one they quietly stop opening:

1. **One source per metric**, chosen deliberately and recorded.
2. **Source shown next to the number.** Any figure should be traceable to where it came
   from, without asking anyone.
3. **Derived numbers labelled as derived.** Organic installs are inferred, not measured, and
   the difference matters when someone challenges the figure.
4. **A gap is a gap.** If a connector has not reported, show that — do not draw a line to
   zero. A missing day rendered as zero is the fastest way to lose trust in a chart.
5. **Re-fetch a rolling window**, so restatements land.
6. **Timezones stated.** Apple reports on its own fiscal calendar, Google on yours,
   analytics on whatever you configured. A day boundary mismatch shifts every comparison by
   a few percent, permanently.

## The short version

- "Installs" means four different things. Pick one source per metric.
- Store proceeds and gross ad earnings are not addable.
- Both stores restate data — re-fetch a window rather than appending a day.
- Judge ASO on organic installs, and label organic as derived.
- Search, browse and referral respond to different work; a single number hides that.
- Never average an average, and always segment conversion rate by source.
- Show the source beside the number, and show gaps as gaps.
